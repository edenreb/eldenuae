Most fitout quotes cover the same short list: partitions, flooring, paint, some joinery. What they leave out is everything that actually determines whether a space opens on time — authority approvals, MEP coordination, kitchen consultant sign-off, snagging, and the hundred small handover items that surface in the last two weeks of any project.

"Turnkey" should mean one contract, one point of accountability, from the first design sketch to the day you get the keys and the space is trading. It shouldn't mean a general contractor who subcontracts design to one firm, MEP to another, and disappears the moment there's a dispute between the two.

## What we mean when we say it

On every project, the same team that draws the space is accountable for building it. That sounds obvious until you've worked with a firm that hands your design off to a separate build partner and stops answering emails the day the contract changes hands.

In practice, turnkey means:

- Design and authority approvals run in parallel, not in sequence, so a DEWA or Civil Defence resubmission doesn't stall the whole programme
- MEP is coordinated by the same team drawing the ceiling plan, not bolted on afterward
- FF&E procurement is tracked against the construction programme, not ordered as an afterthought once the shell is done
- One project manager owns the schedule from kickoff to handover — not a different name every phase

## Why it's rarer than it sounds

Design-only firms have no reason to think about buildability. Build-only contractors have no reason to protect design intent once the drawings are signed off. Splitting the two isn't cheaper — it just moves the cost of coordination onto the client, who ends up doing the project management themselves without realising that's what they signed up for.
